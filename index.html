This page holds everything behind the one-page brief: the data, the charts, the reasoning behind each scope choice and the full list of sources.

The short version: people drink less, yet the industry still grows in value. The money now goes to brands that give people a reason to pay more, and much less to those that simply charge more.

01

Industry overview

Seven international categories worth $277bn, about half of all spirits sold.

Spirits are alcoholic drinks distilled from a fermented base such as grain, grapes, agave, sugar cane, rice or potatoes. Distillation concentrates the alcohol, so most spirits sit between 20% and 50% alcohol by volume (ABV), far above wine or beer. The industry covers distilling, ageing, bottling, brand building and distribution.

This brief covers the seven categories that trade internationally: whisky, brandy and cognac, vodka, tequila, rum, gin and soju. Values are Statista's retail values, taxes included, for drinks bought both in shops and in bars and restaurants.

Why baijiu is out. Adding it would nearly double the market to $545bn, make China a third of world value and put Moutai and Wuliangye at the top of every ranking. The brief would turn into a report on China. It gets its own deep dive further down.

Historical context

1494The Scottish Exchequer Rolls record malt given to Friar John Cor "to make aqua vitae", the first written trace of distilling in Scotland.
1830Aeneas Coffey patents the continuous column still. It runs without stopping between batches and makes a lighter, stronger spirit, which opens the way to large-scale production.
1920–1933US Prohibition. When it ends, states get to write their own rules, and most adopt the three-tier system (producer, wholesaler, retailer) still in place today.
Late 1970sUS drinking peaks: 71% of adults say they drink.
2000s–2010sPremiumisation boom: brands trade drinkers up to more expensive bottles.
2020COVID closes bars. Out-of-home spirits sales fall 31% while at-home sales rise.
2021Peak for the sector on the stock market. Listed alcohol companies have since lost about $830bn of value.
2023–2025Three years in a row of falling global alcohol volumes.
2025Only 54% of US adults drink, the lowest since Gallup started asking in 1939.

03

Geographic segmentation

Three countries hold almost half of the market outside China. Growth is moving south and east.

Who drinks most per person

Europe still drinks the most alcohol per adult (9.2 litres of pure alcohol a year), led by Romania, Georgia and the Czech Republic, against a world average of 5.5 litres. These are also the markets cutting back. Spirits are the largest category of recorded alcohol worldwide and, in much of Asia, most of what people drink.

Where new drinkers come from

  • India is the strongest growth market: total alcohol +4% in 2025, and it should move from 8th to 5th largest alcohol market in the world by 2035. It adds 15–20 million people of legal drinking age every year, and its legal-age population is approaching one billion. Whisky is over half of what Indians drink, and Indian single malt now outsells Scotch single malt there. The catch: taxes, rules and distribution change from state to state.
  • Mexico drinks more tequila again (+3% in 2025) now that cheaper agave lets brands cut prices.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: total alcohol +1% in 2025 and +2% a year to 2035; spirits +6% and RTDs +11%. Local products are 80% of spirits, and small packs are booming. In Kenya Irish whiskey grew 35% and tequila 65%.
  • Türkiye is the key growth market in Europe, driven by the standard price tier: Scotch +12% in 2025, on track to overtake rakı by 2029.
  • The US went the other way: alcohol volumes −5% in 2025, spirits −4%. Cost is the main reason people give for drinking less.

IWSR expects six countries to add the most alcohol volume between 2024 and 2029: India, Mexico, Nigeria, South Africa, Brazil and Ethiopia. China declines overall but keeps pockets of growth, and the US still holds many of the largest single category opportunities.

Why China is left out. Its $140bn market is almost all baijiu, a 40–60% ABV sorghum spirit with its own drinking culture: bought as a gift and downed neat in toasts at business and family banquets, rarely sipped or mixed like Western spirits. See the deep dive.
04

Competitive landscape

A few global groups own the famous brands. Most of the market is still local.

No free source publishes company market shares, so these are estimates: each group's spirits sales, scaled up from producer to retail prices, divided by the market. With a different mark-up the top group's share moves by a couple of points, but the ranking stays the same.

Private label (supermarket own brands such as Kirkland, Aldi or Lidl) is about 8% of spirits value worldwide, larger than any single brand.

The rest, more than half, is a long tail of local brands: Indian whiskies, Philippine rum, Korean soju, Eastern European vodkas.

How players still win

  • Own an occasion. Aperol and the spritz: Campari is the only major group growing.
  • Be where new drinkers are. Pernod Ricard India +19% in Q1 FY27, Diageo India +7%.
  • Earn the premium. Age statements, provenance, real scarcity: Japanese whisky, US ultra-premium tequila.
  • Who falls behind. Single bets on one category in one market: US tequila for Diageo, cognac in China for Martell, Hennessy and Rémy Martin.

Private label

Supermarket brands did not gain from the downturn in Europe: in six EU markets they fell faster than brands. When people trade down in spirits, the brand still matters. In the US, Costco's Kirkland spirits are made by premium producers and sold at much lower mark-ups. Globally, own-label went from 2% of new alcohol launches in 2000 to 8% in 2025.

Sources

Health warnings and labels

  • Ireland will print cancer warnings on every bottle from 2028, the first EU country to do so. The original date was May 2026. Producers expect packaging costs to rise by about a third.
  • US: the Surgeon General's advisory of January 2025 linked alcohol to about 100,000 cancer cases a year and asked for warnings on labels. It is advice, not law. A year later the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines dropped the old limit of one or two drinks a day and now only say "consume less alcohol".

Price

  • Scotland sets a minimum price of 65p per unit of alcohol. Wales raised its own from 50p to 65p on 1 October 2026.

Advertising

  • Banned outright in Norway (since 1975), Turkey (2013) and Lithuania (2018).
  • France, Loi Evin (1991): no TV, cinema or sponsorship, only factual content, a health warning on every ad.
  • By strength: Finland bans ads for drinks above 22% ABV; Sweden bans print ads above 15%.
  • India is a "dark market": alcohol cannot be advertised directly.

Production rules

Scotch must be made and aged in Scotland for at least three years and bottled at 40% ABV or more. Single malt can only leave Scotland in bottles. Duty is not paid while the spirit ages in bond.

06

Innovation and technology

Cheaper inputs, too much stock, greener stills and a slow shift online.

The value chain runs from raw materials to distilling, ageing, bottling, brand building, distribution and finally the shop or the bar. The margin is made in the brand: branded producers earn gross margins of 60–66% and operating margins of 18–29%.

Raw materials: costs are falling

  • Registered agave growers went from 3,180 in 2014 to 42,200 in 2024, and the planted area doubled between 2018 and 2023. Growers say they need about 60 US cents a kilo to cover costs.
  • UK malting barley fell from £200 to £157 a tonne in a year, and the barley area fell 12% in 2026, the lowest in 16 years, as distillers buy less.
  • Glass is the cost that still rises: its price follows natural gas. In India, gas disruption cut bottle output by 35–40% in 2026, and glass is two thirds of packaging, itself a quarter of revenue.

Inventory: too much ageing stock

  • Kentucky holds a record 16.1 million barrels of bourbon, most of which will not be ready before 2030.
  • Jim Beam stopped distilling at its main Clermont distillery for the whole of 2026; bottling continues.
  • In 2025 Diageo paused distilling at several sites (George Dickel, Bulleit's Lebanon distillery, Roe & Co, Teaninich), and Irish Distillers shut Midleton for several months.
  • Scotch exports fell to £5.3bn in 2025 (−1.8%), 1.34bn bottles (−4.3%). India is now the largest market by volume.

Distilling: the real innovation is energy

  • Heat recovery. Mechanical vapour recompression captures the heat in the vapour leaving the still and uses it to heat the next batch. At GlenAllachie it cuts the energy of wash distillation by more than half; the stills there use 60% of all the distillery's energy. Midleton and Aberlour use the same idea.
  • Stored wind power. Annandale is installing a 30 MWh thermal store that turns surplus wind electricity into heat at up to 1,200°C and releases it as steam for the stills.
  • Electric boilers. Pernod Ricard's $250m Jefferson's distillery in Kentucky was designed to distil with electrode boilers instead of gas.
  • Lighter glass. Diageo made a 180g Johnnie Walker bottle (the usual one weighs 850g) and licenses the design to anyone for free.
  • Trading barrels online. Hogshead, a marketplace launched in 2025, lists more than 135,000 barrels of ageing bourbon for licensed buyers.

The industry target: Scotch distilleries net zero by 2040.

Channels: online grows, bars shrink

  • Online went from 2.5% of shop sales in 2018 to 6.1% in 2025 and should reach 8.9% by 2030; 53% of it happens on a phone. Bars, restaurants and hotels fell from 33% of the value of our seven categories in 2019 to 24% in 2020, and have only recovered to 27%. IWSR is more cautious than Statista: it puts e-commerce at about 3.5% of alcohol value, after three years of decline, with China, Brazil and the US driving most of the growth.
  • In the US the three-tier system adds 17–22% at the wholesaler and 25–50% at the retailer, so the shelf price can be 150% above the producer price. RNDC, once the second-largest distributor, is breaking up: it left California in 2025 and is selling its other markets, so power concentrates in Southern Glazer's, Breakthru and Reyes.
  • On-trade value fell 4% in 2025 across 20 markets (restaurants −6%, bars −2%). Bars charge 6.3 times the shop price for value spirits but only 2.3 times for super-premium bottles.

Product innovation

New launches produced 55% of the $231bn of value added in beer, spirits and RTDs over 2015–2024, and the number of launches grows about 5% a year. RTDs are under 5% of alcohol value but about 20% of the value created by innovation. IWSR tracks five kinds of innovation:

Challenges

People are not quitting. They drink less often and choose more carefully.

Health

For the first time, most Americans say moderate drinking is bad for health; among 18–34 year olds it is 66%. Across 15 markets participation is stable at about 76%, but drinks per occasion fell from 4.4 to 3.9 in two years. Boomers are the only group cutting on every measure, and the biggest drag on volume. In 22 major markets, 24% of adults did not drink at all in 2025, up from 19% in 2022.

Social media and culture

Gen Z has not stopped drinking. In IWSR's 15-market survey, its participation rose from 72% (September 2023) to 74% (September 2025), and the gap with all adults narrowed from 9 to 3 points. The enthusiasm for dry days seems to have peaked. It has changed how it drinks: 1.8 categories per occasion, down from 2.8 two years ago, and the habit of mixing drinks on the same night has reversed. It favours cocktails, RTDs and no-alcohol options, and it is the group most likely to bring bars back to life.

The US is the exception, where participation has stalled. A US panel by Attest finds non-drinkers among Gen Z up from 17% to 24% in a year, and far more of them drinking at home. Phones play a part: health content online, socialising on screens and the fear of being filmed all make a big night out less attractive.

GLP-1 drugs

Weight-loss drugs such as Wegovy and Ozempic reduce the urge to drink. In the US, 44% of users drink less after starting treatment and 82% keep the habit after stopping; spirits are cut by 40% of those who reduce. Households with a user spend about 7% less on alcohol after two years. In the UK, users drink on 29% fewer days a week and spend 30% less per visit to a bar or restaurant. Diageo calls the impact "manageable"; Heineken now tells UK pubs to offer half pints and food.

Cannabis drinks

Hemp-derived THC drinks reached $239m in US shops in the year to June 2026, up 135%, and liquor stores are their largest channel. Half of US adults say they would try one. The growth may stop soon: a federal law signed in November 2025 caps hemp products at 0.4mg of THC per container, which covers almost every drink on sale. It was due on 12 November 2026 and has been pushed to 11 December 2026. Retailers and some members of Congress want these drinks regulated like alcohol instead.

Also weighing on the industry

  • Luxury fatigue. Spirits above $100 lost 10% of their value in 2025: China −25%, US −6%; cognac −17%, Scotch −9%.
  • Affordability. Drinks budgets shrank in H1 2025 even among high earners; the winners were standard beer and RTDs.
  • Oversupply. Mature whisky stocks and cheap agave push prices down.
  • Trade wars and health rules (see Regulation).
08

Opportunities

The premium still works, but it has to be earned.

Earned premium

IWSR describes the change as moving "from show it to live it": meaningful moments, verifiable quality and real scarcity count more than limited editions, heavy bottles and celebrity brands. People still pay more, but for a reason: an age statement, a place of origin, a craft they can check. "Fewer but better" is now a real habit: one good cocktail instead of three ordinary drinks.

Occasions

Aperol owns the aperitivo, and the spritz has become "a cultural moment of its own". Aperol grew 3.3% in H1 2026 while the market shrank. Premium drinkers are also going out earlier: 21% go out earlier than a year ago, against 9% who go out later, which favours after-work drinks, early dinners and weekend lunches.

Gifting and travel retail

Airports are where people buy something special for themselves or as a gift. Travel retail value grew 7% in 2025, led by Japanese whisky (+37% in volume) and agave (+10%); cognac fell 11%. Scotch is the largest value opportunity there to 2030, at over $400m.

New formats

Ready-to-drink cocktails overtook vodka in global value in 2025 and grow faster than total alcohol in 8 of the 10 largest markets, mostly because existing drinkers buy them more often rather than because of new buyers. Spirit-based RTDs grow while malt-based ones decline. IWSR's advice is to give each RTD a clear job: a bar-quality cocktail with no effort, a low-ABV spritz for a long afternoon, a stronger drink for intensity. In the US, no- and low-alcohol drinks are expected to grow 13.6% a year to 2029, against 2.1% for traditional alcohol.

Culture as a premium

Soft power has become a premium lever of its own: soju rides the Korean wave, the spritz the Italian lifestyle, agave Mexican culture. As home markets mature, origin itself sells abroad. Soju grew 9% outside Korea in 2025 while falling 2% at home; in the US it should grow 14% a year to 2030, against −1% for spirits overall, as brands reposition it as lighter, fruitier and more premium. The spritz is now "a cultural moment of its own", helped by its colour on social media, draught serves and cans.

09

Market outlook

Flat volume, modest value growth, a different kind of winner.

  • Volume has peaked. To 2030 the market grows about 3% a year in value with flat volume, so every extra dollar must come from price, mix or new drinkers.
  • The premium is changing. Consumers still trade up, but only for a reason: an occasion, provenance or proven quality. Luxury for status alone is losing ground, in China as much as in the West.
  • Growth moves east and south. India, Mexico, the Philippines and Africa add drinkers while Europe, the US and China cut back.
  • Scale is not enough. Campari grew on Aperol while Diageo and Pernod Ricard shrank.
  • Portfolios get smaller. The majors sell non-core brands and put money into RTDs and selected premium categories.

IWSR's own reading for investors is blunt: in mature markets spirits no longer grow in volume, value is the only story left, and in real terms even value is shrinking. What to look for: precision in price tiers, exposure to India, Türkiye, Africa and Latin America, a credible RTD business and a strong position in travel retail.

Deep dive

Baijiu, the market left out

As big as whisky and brandy together, and almost never drunk outside China.

Baijiu is China's national spirit. It is distilled mainly from sorghum, sometimes with wheat or rice, and fermented with a grain starter called qu. It is strong, at 40–60% ABV (Moutai is 53%), and ranges from cheap everyday bottles to luxury ones bought for gifts and business banquets.

Styles are named by aroma: sauce aroma (Moutai), strong aroma (Wuliangye, Luzhou Laojiao) and light aroma (Fenjiu).

How it is drunk

Baijiu is a drink for the table, not the bar. It is poured into small glasses and downed neat in a round of toasts (ganbei) during business dinners, weddings and family banquets, and an expensive bottle is a standard gift. It is rarely sipped slowly, rarely mixed and rarely drunk alone. Western spirits, by contrast, are sipped, mixed into cocktails or bought for the shelf at home. That is why the two markets move for different reasons.

How big it is

The central estimate, about $135bn, starts from the revenue of baijiu producers above a minimum size reported by China's statistics office (RMB 603bn in 2024), converted to dollars and scaled up to retail prices. The 1.6× mark-up comes from comparing Statista's 2022 brand shares for Moutai and Wuliangye with their actual revenue. In Statista's 2022 brand ranking, Moutai (5%) and Wuliangye (4%) are the two largest spirits brands in the world by value.

Why it is shrinking

  • Official meals went dry. On 18 May 2025 the government banned alcohol, expensive dishes and cigarettes from all official working meals. Official consumption was once up to 60% of Moutai's revenue; it is now under 5% of the industry's.
  • Banquets got smaller. One Guangzhou merchant says banquet sales are a third of their peak; hosts trade down from XO cognac to VSOP or cheap whisky.
  • The leaders are falling. Moutai's 2025 revenue fell 1.2% and profit 4.5%, its first fall in both since listing in 2001. Wuliangye reported a much sharper drop in 2025.
  • The engine has switched off. China's alcohol volume fell 4% in 2025 (−2% without baijiu). Business dining, once the engine of premium sales, is largely gone, and IWSR does not expect it to return.
  • A new way of drinking. Young Chinese drink for enjoyment rather than obligation: casual occasions, value, home consumption, smaller bottles, delivery apps, drinking alone or in small groups, in bistros, cocktail bars and live-music venues. They are largely absent from expensive brown spirits. The growth pockets are imported white spirits, RTDs and, increasingly, whisky.
So what. China confirms the thesis of the brief from the other side of the world: spirits bought as a status symbol, for gifts and business dinners, are exactly what has stopped selling.
Method

How the numbers were built

Market

The market is the sum of Statista's whisky, brandy, vodka, tequila, rum, gin and soju segments, for drinks bought in shops and in bars and restaurants, at retail prices including taxes, converted to dollars at each year's average exchange rate. Litres per person divide this volume by the population implied by Statista's own per-person figures (about 7.8bn people). Price per litre is value divided by volume.

Company shares

Company sales are reported at producer prices, while Statista measures the market at retail prices. Each group's spirits sales are multiplied by a retail mark-up of 2.0× and divided by $277bn. The mark-up is uncertain: Statista's own brand shares for Moutai and Wuliangye imply 1.4–1.8×, while US three-tier mark-ups imply more than 2×. With 1.6× the top eight groups hold about 28%; with 2.2× about 38%. The ranking does not change. Bacardi (about $6bn) and Suntory Global Spirits (about $5.5bn) do not publish spirits sales, so their figures are estimates. Diageo includes RTDs but not beer. Moët Hennessy includes cognac and other spirits only, not champagne. Exchange rates: €1 = $1.13, RMB 7.2 = $1. Private label uses Statista's 2022 brand share of all spirits.

Baijiu

Three methods: producer revenue from China's statistics office scaled to retail (about $134bn), the industry association's output value scaled the same way (about $177bn, an upper bound), and Statista's at-home spirits revenue for China, almost all baijiu ($140bn). Central estimate: $135bn.
